Getting started

Download, install, first scan
Do I need the Apple Silicon or the Intel download?
If your Mac has an M-series chip (M1 or later, roughly 2020 onward), grab the Apple Silicon build; older Macs take the Intel build. Not sure? Open  menu → About This Mac — the "Chip" line says Apple M-something, the "Processor" line says Intel. Both builds are the same v1.0.0 app, natively compiled for each architecture, and the download page tries to detect the right one for you.
Which versions of macOS does Spectra support?
Spectra requires macOS 12 Monterey or later — that covers Monterey, Ventura, Sonoma, Sequoia and newer. We target Monterey as the floor because it's the oldest release with the file-system and permission APIs Spectra relies on for fast, safe scanning. Anything older, unfortunately, can't run it.
Can I try Spectra without scanning my real disk?
Yes — Spectra ships with a built-in demo mode that runs on a realistic sample disk, right in your browser view of the app. You can click through the treemap, run a pretend Smart Clean and explore every feature without touching a single real file. It's included in the free download, with nothing to sign up for.
macOS says the app "can't be opened". What do I do?
That's Gatekeeper being cautious about apps downloaded outside the App Store. The fix takes ten seconds: right-click (or Control-click) Spectra in Applications → Open → Open in the confirmation dialog. macOS remembers your choice and never asks again. On newer macOS versions you can also approve it under System Settings → Privacy & Security → Open Anyway. Never disable Gatekeeper globally — you don't need to.

Privacy & safety

What Spectra can — and can't — touch
Does Spectra phone home?
No. Spectra needs no account, sends no telemetry, no analytics, no crash reports, no "anonymized usage data". Scanning, hashing, cleaning and report generation all happen on your Mac, and the results stay there. Unplug the network cable and every feature keeps working — that's the honest test, and Spectra passes it.
Is it safe? Could Spectra delete something important?
Spectra is built around three guardrails. First, it never deletes anything on its own — every item is listed for your review and needs an explicit confirm. Second, deletes are Trash-only: nothing is ever hard-deleted, so anything can be restored with one click until you empty the Trash yourself. Third, ignore lists let you fence off folders Spectra must never propose. System-critical paths are excluded from cleaning suggestions entirely.
What is "System Data" and why is it so huge?
"System Data" in macOS storage settings is a catch-all bucket: app caches, logs, browser profiles, Time Machine local snapshots, virtual machine images, developer junk and anything else macOS didn't classify. That's exactly what Spectra's Disk X-Ray untangles — instead of one opaque grey bar, you get a treemap where every gigabyte has a name and a folder you can open. Most of the "mystery" usually turns out to be caches and developer leftovers, which Smart Clean and Dev Cleaner handle.
I emptied the Trash but my disk is still full. Why?
Two usual suspects. First, APFS local snapshots: macOS keeps point-in-time copies of your disk (mostly for Time Machine), so deleted files can live on inside a snapshot until macOS ages it out — usually within 24 hours. Second, purgeable space: macOS reports some space as used but reclaims it automatically when needed. Spectra's Storage Timeline makes this visible — you'll see free space climb over the hours after a big clean instead of instantly.

Cleaning

How the reclaiming actually works
How does duplicate detection work?
In two passes. Spectra first groups files by exact size — a cheap filter that rules out most of the disk instantly. Surviving candidates are then read and compared by cryptographic hash, so a match means the files are byte-for-byte identical. There is no "similar-looking photo" guesswork and no fuzzy matching: if Spectra calls something a duplicate, it is one. You pick which copy to keep; the rest go to the Trash.
Is Dev Cleaner safe? What about my node_modules?
Dev Cleaner only targets artifacts your tools rebuild on demand: Xcode DerivedData and old simulator runtimes, node_modules folders in projects you haven't touched in months, dangling Docker images, and package-manager caches (npm, yarn, pnpm, Homebrew, CocoaPods). Deleting a node_modules regenerates completely with one npm install — your source code and lockfiles are never touched. Everything is itemized per project before you confirm, and active projects can be added to the ignore list.
What exactly does Smart Clean remove?
Five reviewed categories: user app caches, old logs, browser junk (stale caches and leftover profiles from browsers you've removed), forgotten installers (.dmg and .pkg files in Downloads), and stale iOS device backups. Each category is pre-scanned and itemized with sizes; nothing is pre-selected behind your back, and everything goes to the Trash after your confirm — never straight to oblivion.
How is Spectra different from DaisyDisk or CleanMyMac?
DaisyDisk is a beautiful visual analyzer; CleanMyMac is a subscription cleaning suite. Spectra is deliberately both, plus a monitor: an interactive treemap and sunburst analyzer, a full cleaning toolkit (Smart Clean, Dev Cleaner, hash-verified duplicates, uninstaller, startup manager), and live system stats with a storage timeline — in one app, 100% local, for a one-time $11.99 instead of a yearly fee. If you're a developer, the dedicated Dev Cleaner alone usually pays the difference in reclaimed gigabytes.
